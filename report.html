Cabaka Credit — Care-to-Home Copilot

AI agents that stop a medical bill from becoming a mortgage denial
Author: Carson Tavares  ·  AI Foundations Final Project, October 2026
GitHub repo: github.com/CarsonAoTavares/cabaka-credit
Live app: cabaka-credit.onrender.com (free hosting; first load after idle can take ~50 s)
One sentence: At a clinic front desk, Cabaka's agents design a medical payment plan a patient can truly afford and that keeps them mortgage-ready; at a community bank, the same agents underwrite a small rural mortgage at a fraction of today's origination cost, with a human loan officer approving every decision.

2. Motivation

"My mother sold her home and lived on one meal a day to send me to America for school. When her hands finally needed surgery, the only medical financing we were offered would have erased everything she'd built, disqualifying us to access home mortgages." — Carson Tavares, founder

Who has this problem. Low-income, working-class, immigrant and rural families: patients delaying care, and first-time buyers who need a small mortgage (<$150k). In New York that means the Bronx, Queens, Brooklyn and Staten Island; nationally it means rural counties where small homes are common.

How it works today, and what it costs. The clinic, the bank and the family each see one piece:

  • At the clinic, patients are offered a 0%-promo medical credit card underwritten on a credit score, not on whether they can pay. CareCredit charges 26.99% during a 6–24 month promo; if any balance is left at the end, all of that interest is charged [3]. Patients paid about $1 billion in deferred interest on medical cards and loans in 2018–2020 [4]. For our fictional patient Maria, a $6,800 surgery paid at the $450/month she can afford triggers $1,835 of retroactive interest (output of the app's simulate_deferred_interest_card tool).
  • On the credit report, medical collections sat on 43 million credit reports, about $88 billion, and about 1 in 5 households report medical debt [1]. Americans owe at least $220 billion in medical debt overall [2]. Medical debt is a poor predictor of repayment [1][5], yet the CFPB rule that would have removed it from credit reports was vacated by a federal court in July 2025 [6]. Lenders still see it.
  • At the bank, 30% of would-be rural homebuyers were denied for credit history vs. 19% urban; for small mortgages, credit scores were the leading cause of denial; and 28% of rural Southerners have medical bills in collections vs. 17% elsewhere [5]. Only 26% of low-cost homes are bought with a mortgage, versus 71% of higher-cost homes [7]. One reason: originating any retail mortgage cost about $11,600 in Q3 2023 [8], so a $112k loan earning ~3% loses the lender money.

Why care. For these families a single hospital visit quietly becomes a housing denial years later. The CFPB itself has asked technologists to "use data and technology to improve access to credit and reduce the origination cost of small dollar loans" [9].

3. What is new here

Existing productWhat it doesWhat it misses
CareCredit (Synchrony), AccessOnePoint-of-care medical credit; deferred-interest promosScore-based, not cash-flow based; the deferred-interest trap; no view of the patient's housing goals

ChatGPT / Claude chatCan explain loansDoes arithmetic in its head, can be prompt-injected, no access to the borrower's data, no audit trail or policy enforcement

What Cabaka does differently. (1) It treats the medical bill and the mortgage as one credit pipeline: every medical offer is scored by whether the family stays mortgage-ready (project_mortgage_readiness). (2) One agentic rail, two products: the same Underwriter, Offer Architect and Compliance agents run a clinic loan or a community-bank mortgage, swapping only policy text and tools. That shared rail is what drives origination cost down. (3) It underwrites on cash flow and the medical-excluded score. (4) A code Verifier checks that every dollar figure the borrower reads came from a calculator, not the model. Honest caveat: each piece exists somewhere. The combination, aimed at small-loan borrowers that the market ignores, is the new part.

4. The agents

AgentJobTools (medical / mortgage)
GatekeeperScreens the free-text notes (the only text a stranger can type) for prompt injection and writes a neutral summary. A regex pre-filter runs first, at zero cost.Structured JSON output only, no tools
UnderwriterUnderwrites on cash flow and the medical-excluded score; outputs decision, risk tier, max affordable monthly payment.analyze_cash_flow, get_credit_report, lookup_procedure_benchmark, check_financial_assistance / compute_mortgage_ratios, check_program_eligibility, get_county_lending_context
Offer ArchitectBuilds 2–3 options and compares total cost, worst case and mortgage readiness.amortize, simulate_deferred_interest_card, project_mortgage_readiness / amortize, estimate_origination_cost
Compliance & ExplainerPolicy and fair-lending review; 180-word letter in the borrower's language; officer summary.submit_review
Verifier (code)Recomputes the payment; checks every $ and % in the letter against tool outputs; enforces payment ≤ 40% of residual income, APR ≤ 18%, no deferred-interest product; blocks prompt leaks and prohibited-basis words.—

Why this organisation. A fixed pipeline (not a free-roaming manager agent) because lending must be auditable and repeatable: every run takes the same steps in the same order. Each agent only gets the tools for its step, so the Underwriter cannot "design" an offer and the Explainer cannot run calculators. All arithmetic lives in Python tools; the LLM decides what to call and how to explain it.

Models. Claude Opus 5.5 at low effort for the three reasoning agents: they must pick tools well, follow lending policy, and write clearly in Spanish or English. Claude Haiku 5.5 for the Gatekeeper: a yes/no screen that runs on every request, at $0.10 per million input tokens. Section 5 tests whether cheaper choices hurt quality.

Note what the agents did not do: they did not simply sell Maria a loan. The cheapest path for her (free care) earns the lender nothing, and the agents recommended it anyway, because the policy puts the borrower's mortgage readiness first.

In context: $0.12 of AI per application, against an industry cost to originate a mortgage of about $11,600 [8]. Even at 10,000 users, model cost is a rounding error next to loan economics; the real costs at scale would be hosting, data (bureau pulls, bank-account connections) and compliance staff. Hosting today is Render's free plan; production would need a paid instance.

What I did to keep cost down

  • Code does the math. Tools return small JSON summaries (e.g. 90 days of transactions become 9 numbers), so the model never reads raw data.
  • Prompt caching. The system prompt and tool list are cached, and so is the growing conversation inside each agent's loop. Measured: 12,756 of ~21,100 input tokens per run are cache reads. Without caching the same runs would cost $0.168 instead of $0.124 (−26%).
  • Right-sized models. Haiku for screening; Opus at low effort for reasoning.
  • Hard caps. Max 8 turns per agent, 150k tokens per run, $5/day budget, 6 runs per visitor per 10 minutes.

Did cost-cutting hurt quality?

I ran the same 4 applicants (Maria, Dale, Kwame, Tina) under four settings: 16 real runs plus one retry, all logged in docs/evidence/model_comparison.json. Then I read every recommendation by hand for judgment errors that the automatic checks cannot see.

SettingCost / runTimeHard checks passedJudgment errors found on review
A. Opus 5.5, low effort (shipped)$0.11945 s4 / 40. Charity care first for Maria and Dale; price negotiation and a sliding-fee clinic first for Kwame (quote above fair price)
B. Opus 5.5, medium effort$0.157 (+31%)62 s4 / 41. Kwame's max payment was set to $0, yet a $166 loan was offered. Same recommendations as A otherwise: more cost, no gain
C. Sonnet 5.5, low effort$0.060 (−49%)36 s4 / 42. Kwame was steered to a 12% loan before trying to lower an above-fair-price quote; Tina's "max affordable payment" was set to her whole residual income ($1,611), not the 43% DTI rule
D. Haiku 5.5 for every agent$0.0045 (−96%)32 s4 / 42 serious. It labelled Maria and Dale "decline" despite strong cash flow, then recommended help for them anyway (a contradictory record an officer could act on). Maria's fallback loan was priced at the 18% APR cap, where Opus used 9% or 0%

Evidence that cost-cutting hurt: every setting passed the code checks, so the cheap models look fine on a dashboard. But the cheaper the model, the more judgment errors appeared: errors in who gets declined and what gets recommended first, which are exactly what lending regulators scrutinize. So I kept Opus but cut cost where quality held: low effort (B shows medium adds 31% cost for nothing), Haiku only for the yes/no Gatekeeper, and caching (−26%). Sonnet is a credible next step if a fixed "price-check first" rule is added to the Verifier. One caveat: 4 applicants is a small sample, so this is directional, not statistical.

8. Limits and next steps

  • Synthetic data. Applicants, procedure price ranges and county incomes are illustrative. Next: Plaid-style bank data, a real bureau pull, CMS price-transparency files and HMDA county data.
  • Simplified program rules. USDA/FHA/HomeReady screening is approximate; a loan officer confirms.
  • One number is still computed by the model. The Underwriter works out the "40% of residual income" payment cap itself, and in one run the Compliance agent flagged exactly that ("underwriting worked it out by hand"). Next: a max_affordable_payment tool, so the Verifier can check it too.
  • Judgment checks are manual. The comparison in Section 5 found errors the code checks miss (e.g. "decline" for a creditworthy applicant). Next: add consistency rules to the Verifier (no "decline" when residual income is positive and an offer is recommended; price check before any loan).
  • Network failures stop a run. One comparison run hit an API connection error; the app logged it and stopped cleanly, but it does not retry the whole step yet.
  • No servicing yet. The writing assignment's third engine (monitor and intervene before default) is not built.
  • Fair-lending testing. Needs disparate-impact testing on real outcomes before any production use.
  • Next month: a pilot with one clinic and one community bank; a servicing agent; a Spanish voice interface.
